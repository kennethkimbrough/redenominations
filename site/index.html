Currency redenominations, 1945 to today

Zeros Removed

When money loses so much value that the numbers stop fitting on a banknote, a country prints a new currency and strikes zeros off the old one. Hungary struck 29 in a single day in 1946. This page lists every such reform since 1945 and chains them together, so you can ask what a banknote from any year is worth in the unit a country uses now, and what that comes to in US dollars at today's exchange rate.

    A chain is verified when every step has a checked citation and each step's new currency is the next step's old one. The tests in the repository refuse anything else. Partial chains show what is still missing.

    Method and sources

    • The table of reforms is transcribed from the Wikipedia article on redenomination (read 30 October 2026) and kept as data in the repository, so it can be checked and corrected row by row.
    • Euro conversion rates come from Council Regulation (EC) No 2866/98, Article 1.
    • "Old per new" is how many units of the old currency bought one unit of the new one. A rate below one means the new unit was smaller.
    • Today's dollar figure uses a published exchange rate fetched once a day by a scheduled job (date and source shown with the result). It is the official or market rate the source publishes; in countries with a parallel street rate, the two can differ a lot.
    • The converter multiplies the rates along the chain. It says nothing about purchasing power: a million pengő in 1945 and the forint it converts to today bought very different things.
    • Steps marked "not yet cited" are included so the chain reads correctly, but the country stays partial until they are checked against a primary source.
